# Mini-Series: **Robotics Foundations for Kids (Age 8–10)**
_A playful intro to building and thinking like robots_

## **1. What Is a Robot?**
- What makes something a robot (sense–think–act)
- Real-world examples: home, school, space
- Safety and kindness when using robots

### Overview
When you hear the word “robot,” you might imagine a shiny metal helper rolling around and talking in beeps. But a robot doesn’t have to look like a person, and it doesn’t even have to talk. The heart of robotics is a simple idea: sense, think, act. If a machine can sense the world, think about what the sensor data means, and act in a way that changes the world, then it fits our everyday definition of a robot.

Let’s break that down:

- Sense: This is how a robot gathers information. Just like your eyes, ears, and skin help you understand your surroundings, a robot uses sensors for light, sound, touch, distance, color, temperature, and more. A vacuum robot uses bump sensors to feel when it touches a wall. A line-following robot uses a light sensor to see a dark line on a white floor.
- Think: Once a robot has data, it needs a brain to decide what to do. The “brain” can be a simple program with rules like “if you touch a wall, turn left,” or it can be a more advanced program that learns patterns (like recognizing voices or faces). In kid-friendly robots, this thinking usually happens through simple commands, sequences, and decisions we write using block-based coding, such as Scratch or MakeCode.
- Act: Finally, a robot uses motors, wheels, arms, lights, or speakers to do something. Acting includes moving forward, turning, picking up objects, flashing lights, beeping, or sending a message to a computer. Action is the visible part of robotics and often the most fun—this is where you see the robot come alive.

If you’ve used a motion-activated soap dispenser, watched an automatic door slide open, or set up a smart speaker to answer a question, you’ve already met robots and robotic devices. Robots appear in homes (robot vacuums), schools (coding kits and simple bots), hospitals (telepresence or surgery assistants), farms (tractor guidance), factories (arm robots that assemble cars), and space (rovers like Curiosity and Perseverance). Some robots are big and powerful; others are tiny and precise. What connects them is that they all follow sense–think–act.

It’s important to remember the difference between a robot and a regular machine. A blender is a machine: you press a button, and it spins. It doesn’t sense the fruit or think about how smooth to make it—it just acts. A robot, however, might sense the chunkiness and automatically adjust speed until your smoothie is perfect. The ability to make decisions based on sensed information is what adds the “smart” to robotics.

Another cool idea in robotics is autonomy. An autonomous robot can make choices without a person pressing a button every time. Think of a robot vacuum: you turn it on, and it explores the house by itself. It doesn’t need you to tell it to turn left at the couch; it senses the couch, thinks “there’s an obstacle,” and acts by turning. Some robots are fully autonomous, some are remote-controlled by humans, and many are a mix of both.

Robotics doesn’t require fancy parts to get started. You can build simple “robots” from cardboard, rubber bands, and tape to explore how movement and decision-making work. You can pretend senses with paper “sensor” icons, write rules on sticky notes (your program), and act by pushing or pulling parts. Later, you can swap pretend sensors for real ones and sticky notes for block code.

Now let’s talk about safety and kindness. Robots follow rules we give them, so we must choose rules that keep people and pets safe. Good rules include turning off motors if a foot or tail is nearby, moving slowly in crowded areas, and never pointing fast-moving parts toward someone’s face. Kindness in robotics also means using robots to help and not harm: a robot that helps a classmate hand out papers is kind; a robot that tries to trip someone is not. Respecting privacy is part of kindness too: if your robot uses a camera, ask permission and explain what you’re doing.

Robotics also teaches teamwork and creativity. When you build a robot in a group, one person might design the body, another writes the code, another tests sensors, and someone else records what works and what doesn’t. Together, you solve problems like “Why does our robot turn too early?” or “How can we make it stop at a red marker?” You’ll try ideas, test, learn, fix, and try again. This loop—plan, build, test, improve—mirrors how real engineers work.

As you explore robotics, keep asking the “big three” questions:

1) What will our robot sense?
2) How will it think about that information?
3) What action will it take?

If you can answer those clearly, your project will feel doable and fun. And as you grow, you’ll learn to add more sensors, smarter thinking, and smoother actions, turning simple bots into impressive little machines.

### Try-It Ideas
- Draw a “robot’s day” comic showing sense–think–act moments.
- Make a cardboard or LEGO bot with pretend sensors (colored stickers) and write rule cards: “If see red, stop. If see blue, turn.” Act out the program.
- Observe a real device at home (automatic faucet, soap dispenser, thermostat). Identify what it senses, how it thinks, and what it does.

### Questions
1) In your own words, explain sense–think–act with a real-life example.
2) What makes a robot different from a regular machine like a blender?
3) Why are safety and kindness important rules when building or using robots?

## **2. Simple Machines & Movement**
- Wheels, levers, gears, pulleys in robots
- How motion, speed, and force work
- Build challenge: make a paper or LEGO lever bot

### Overview
Robots move by combining simple machines—basic tools that change force and motion—into clever designs. Understanding these machines helps you build robots that roll smoothly, lift objects, or turn precisely. The classic simple machines are the wheel and axle, lever, pulley, inclined plane (ramp), wedge, and screw. In robotics, we most often use wheels and axles, levers, pulleys, and gears, plus links and joints to create arms and grippers.

Start with the wheel and axle. A wheel lets a robot roll with less friction (rubbing resistance) than sliding. The axle is the rod that goes through the wheel’s center. When a motor turns the axle, the wheel turns too, and the robot moves. Bigger wheels cover more distance per turn (higher speed for the same motor rotation), but they can make starting and stopping less precise. Smaller wheels give fine control and stronger pushing power (torque) but move less distance per rotation.

Levers help a robot lift or press with less effort. A lever is a bar that rotates around a point called a fulcrum. If the fulcrum is closer to the load and farther from the effort, you can lift heavy things more easily. In robots, levers appear in grippers, arms, and linkages (connected levers). By changing where the fulcrum sits and how long each side is, you can trade speed for strength. Longer effort arms move farther and faster but use more space; shorter effort arms move less but can push harder.

Pulleys let robots change the direction of a force or lift smoothly. A pulley is a wheel with a groove; a string or belt runs in the groove. By looping the string through multiple pulleys, you can multiply your lifting power. In small robots, belt and pulley systems are common for moving wheels or arms safely; belts can slip a little, which protects parts from breaking when something jams.

Gears are toothed wheels that mesh with each other to transfer motion. If a small gear drives a big gear, the robot’s output turns slower but with more torque (stronger pushing power). If a big gear drives a small gear, the output turns faster but with less torque. Gear ratios help you tune a robot: heavy loads need big output gears; quick spinning needs small output gears. Spur gears (straight teeth) are common; worm gears can lock movement when the motor is off; bevel gears change direction.

Inclined planes, wedges, and screws show up less in kid robots but are great to understand. An inclined plane (ramp) lets robots climb gradually instead of jumping up. A wedge splits objects or helps a robot slide under something. A screw is like a ramp wrapped around a cylinder; turning a screw converts rotation into forward motion—handy for lifts.

To control motion, think about speed and force. Speed tells how fast something moves; force tells how hard it pushes. Torque is a twisting force that turns wheels and gears. Motors provide torque; simple machines shape that torque to get the motion you want. When building, ask: Do we need speed (cover distance fast) or strength (push or lift)? Choose wheel size and gear ratios accordingly.

Balance and stability matter. A robot with a high center of gravity (its “weight center” up high) tips easily, especially during turns or on ramps. Keep heavy parts low and centered. Wide wheelbases (wheels spaced apart) help stability. Adding a caster—a small free-rolling wheel—can prevent tipping while turning.

Friction can be your friend or enemy. You need enough friction between wheel and floor to push forward; too little and wheels spin without going anywhere. But too much friction in bearings or on rough surfaces wastes energy and slows you down. Lubricated bearings and smooth axles reduce friction so parts turn freely.

When you test movement, change only one thing at a time—wheel size, gear ratio, weight placement—so you can see what actually made it better. Record observations: “With 40mm wheels, robot turned sharply but couldn’t climb the ramp. With 60mm wheels, it climbed but turns were wider.” This kind of simple engineering note helps you build smarter.

### Try-It Ideas
- Build a lever gripper from craft sticks and brads; experiment with different fulcrum positions to see strength vs. speed.
- Make a small car with two gear setups: fast/low-torque and slow/high-torque. Race on flat ground and climb a book ramp.
- Tape a coin to different parts of the robot’s body; observe how balance changes turning and climbing.

### Questions
1) How do gears trade speed for torque in a robot drivetrain?
2) Why does a low center of gravity improve a robot’s stability?
3) Describe a lever in a robot arm and how moving the fulcrum changes its performance.

## **3. Sensors: How Robots Feel the World**
- Light, touch, sound, distance sensors
- Input vs. output explained simply
- Try-it: line-following with paper tracks

### Overview
Sensors are a robot’s super senses. They turn real-world signals—light, sound, touch, distance, color, motion—into numbers the robot can understand. With those numbers, the robot decides what to do next. If a sensor says “too close,” the robot backs up. If a sensor says “bright line detected,” the robot follows it. Learning sensors is like learning to listen carefully to the world and translate it into simple, useful information.

Common kid-friendly sensors include:

- Touch (bump) sensors: These tell whether the robot touched something. They’re like a light switch: off (no bump) or on (bump). Great for preventing crashes.
- Light/Color sensors: These measure how much light is hitting the sensor or identify colors. Perfect for line-following or detecting colored markers that mean “stop” or “turn.”
- Distance sensors: Ultrasonic sensors use sound waves to estimate how far away objects are. Infrared distance sensors use invisible light. Both help robots avoid obstacles.
- Sound (microphone) sensors: These can detect loudness or respond to claps and voices. Many beginner kits use simple sound thresholds (“if loud > 70, start”).
- Gyro/accelerometer: These measure rotation and motion. They help a robot drive straight, turn precisely, or detect if it tips.

Input vs. output is a key idea. Sensors are inputs—they bring information into the robot. Motors, lights, speakers, and screens are outputs—they do something based on the information and program rules. If we think in terms of a flow: input (sensor) → think (program) → output (action). That flow keeps your robot organized and predictable.

Robot sensor data isn’t perfect. Sometimes a light sensor reads “line” when it’s actually a shadow. Distance sensors can bounce off shiny surfaces in strange ways. So robots use calibration and thresholds to make better decisions. Calibration means measuring sensor readings in known conditions and setting your program to match. For a light sensor, you might record numbers on white paper (maybe 80–90) and on the black line (maybe 20–30). Then set a threshold (maybe 50): if reading < 50, you’re on the line; otherwise, you’re off the line.

Sampling is how often the robot reads a sensor. Reading too slowly might miss fast changes (like quick turns). Reading too quickly can make your robot jittery if the signal is noisy. Most beginner robots read sensors many times per second and average several readings to reduce noise.

Placement matters. Where a sensor sits on the robot changes what it “sees.” A light sensor too high won’t detect the line well; too low might scrape the floor. Distance sensors work best when pointed straight ahead and not blocked by parts of the robot’s frame. If your robot acts strangely, try moving the sensor position.

Line following is a classic beginner challenge. The robot looks for a dark line on a lighter floor. A simple program might say: if you see the line (reading < threshold), turn slightly left; if you don’t, turn right until you find it. More advanced versions use two sensors (left and right) and adjust motor speeds to stay centered.

Touch-based navigation is another good starting project. You can drive forward until the bump sensor is pressed, then back up and turn before trying again. The touch sensor protects your robot and teaches cause-and-effect: bump → change plan.

As you test sensors, take notes: “In bright sunlight, the light sensor reads higher. We lowered our threshold by 5.” Or “Distance sensor saw the shiny chair as farther than it really was. We moved it 2cm up and added a slow approach.” Little observations help you make strong improvements.

### Try-It Ideas
- Calibrate a light sensor: write down readings on white and black, pick a threshold, and test line-following on a paper track.
- Use an ultrasonic sensor to measure different objects: plush toy, metal can, book stack. Compare readings and adjust position.
- Create a “bump to turn” maze: drive forward until touch sensor triggers, then back 10cm and turn 30°.

### Questions
1) Why do robots need thresholds for sensors like light or sound?
2) How does sensor placement affect a robot’s behavior? Give an example.
3) Explain input vs. output using a line-following robot.

## **4. Programming Basics**
- Commands, sequences, loops, and events
- Debugging: finding and fixing “bugs”
- Block coding with Scratch or MakeCode

### Overview
Programming is how you tell a robot what to do. Think of it like writing a recipe: clear steps, in order, with rules for what happens next. Kid-friendly robots often use block coding—drag-and-drop commands that snap together—so you can focus on logic instead of spelling or punctuation. The building blocks of robot programs are commands, sequences, loops, events, and decisions.

- Commands: One instruction, like “move forward for 1 second” or “turn on the light.”
- Sequences: Putting commands in order: first do A, then B, then C. Robots follow steps exactly, so order matters.
- Loops: Repeat commands many times, either a set number (“repeat 10 times”) or forever (“keep checking the sensor and adjust”). Loops make behaviors continuous, like driving until you reach a goal.
- Events: Something that triggers code, like “when button pressed” or “when loud sound detected.” Events let your robot respond to the world.
- Decisions: Also called conditionals, they let the robot choose: “if distance < 20cm, then stop; else, keep going.” Decisions are the robot’s thinking.

You’ll also use variables—named boxes that store numbers or words. For example, you might store the latest light sensor reading in a variable called `lightValue` and compare it to your threshold. Variables help you remember information between steps.

Good programs start with simple plans. Write out what success looks like: “Our robot should follow the line and stop at the red square.” Then break it down: “Read light sensors; adjust motors; check for red; stop if red found.” Imagine what could go wrong and add safety: “If sensor reads nonsense, slow down.”

Debugging is finding and fixing bugs—surprising behaviors you didn’t want. Maybe the robot won’t stop at red, or it wobbles too much. Debugging works best when you change one thing at a time and test. You can add `print` messages or show sensor values on a small screen to see what’s happening. If a robot turns left when you expected right, check your sign: maybe you subtracted instead of added.

Decomposition means breaking big problems into smaller ones. For a delivery robot, you could create separate blocks: `findLine()`, `followLine()`, `stopAtMarker()`, `avoidObstacle()`. Testing each piece makes the whole robot stronger.

Another helpful idea is state—what mode the robot is in right now. Common states might be “exploring,” “following line,” or “stopped.” You can write code that changes behavior based on the state. This is called a finite state machine (FSM): the robot moves between states when certain events happen, like “if bump sensed, switch from exploring to avoidObstacle.”

Writing pseudocode—plain-language steps—before coding saves time:

1) Start
2) Set threshold = 50
3) Loop: read sensors
4) If left sees line and right doesn’t, turn slightly left
5) If right sees line and left doesn’t, turn slightly right
6) If both see line, go straight
7) If red found, stop and cheer

Then translate into blocks. If you get confused, return to the pseudocode and check each step.

### Try-It Ideas
- Make an event-driven robot: “When clap detected, toggle motors on/off.”
- Build a simple FSM: press button to cycle states (explore → follow line → stop), show current state on LEDs.
- Write pseudocode for an obstacle avoider, then code it and test.

### Questions
1) Explain how loops and events work together in a robot program.
2) What is a state machine, and why is it useful for robotics?
3) Describe a debugging step you’d use when a robot turns the wrong way.

## **5. Control Systems: From Plan to Action**
- Decision-making: if/then, rules, and goals
- Open vs. closed loop (with kid examples)
- Calibrating a sensor to get better results

### Overview
A control system is the part of your robot that turns goals into specific actions. If your goal is “drive straight,” the control system decides how much power to send to each motor to keep the robot on track. If your goal is “stay 20cm from a wall,” the control system reads the distance sensor and adjusts movement to maintain that spacing. Control systems make robots precise, reliable, and safe.

At the simplest level, you can write rule-based decisions: “if distance < 20cm, stop; else, go forward.” This is an open-loop approach if you just do it once and trust it will be fine. Open-loop means you don’t check the result after acting—you simply send the command. Open-loop is ok for easy tasks: move forward 1 second, play a sound, flash a light.

Closed-loop control adds feedback: the robot senses the result of its action and corrects if needed. For example, to drive straight, read a gyro sensor to see if you’ve drifted left or right. If you drift left, increase right motor power a little; if you drift right, increase left motor power. You keep sensing and correcting many times per second. That loop—sense, compare to goal, adjust—makes robots accurate.

The “goal” in a control system is called a setpoint. The robot measures its current value (like distance or angle), compares it to the setpoint, and computes the error: error = setpoint – current. If error is positive, you need to do more; if negative, do less. Even simple if/then rules can use error thinking.

For smoother control, engineers use proportional control: adjust power in proportion to the error. Big error → bigger correction; small error → smaller correction. For kids, you can think “the farther I am from the line, the more I turn back toward it.” More advanced systems add integral (remembering past error) and derivative (reacting to the rate of change), often called PID control. You don’t need deep math to start: just test different correction amounts until your robot follows nicely without wobbling.

Calibration improves control. If your distance sensor reads 18cm when a ruler says 20cm, you can add a tiny offset or tune your thresholds to compensate. If one motor is slightly stronger than the other, you can reduce its power by a small percentage to balance. Record these adjustments so your team remembers what worked.

Timing matters. How often should your robot read sensors and adjust? Fast loops (many times per second) react quickly but can make robots jittery if readings are noisy. Slower loops are calmer but may miss quick changes. Try a few rates and pick what works best for your task.

Decision trees and flowcharts help plan control: start at the top (“goal: deliver to red square”), branch on conditions (“if red seen → stop; else → keep following line”), and include safety (“if bump → back up”). Drawing these before coding makes your program clearer.

### Try-It Ideas
- Drive-straight challenge: use a gyro; adjust motor powers proportionally to angle error; compare with open-loop driving.
- Wall-follow challenge: maintain ~20cm from a wall using distance sensor; tune correction strength to avoid oscillation.
- Flowchart a delivery task; implement it with clear states and safety rules.

### Questions
1) What’s the difference between open-loop and closed-loop control? Give a kid-friendly example of each.
2) How does proportional control help a robot follow a line or drive straight?
3) Why is calibration important for control systems, and how would you calibrate a distance sensor?

## **6. Power & Structure**
- Batteries and safe power use
- Frames, chassis, and strong builds
- Balance and stability (why bots tip!)

### Overview
Power and structure are the hidden heroes of robotics. Power gives your robot energy to move and think. Structure gives your robot a strong, safe body to carry parts and handle bumps. When power and structure are designed well, your robot runs longer, moves smoother, and survives real-world adventures.

Let’s start with power. Beginner robots often use batteries. Three ideas matter most: voltage (electric “pressure”), current (electric flow), and capacity (how much energy the battery stores, often measured in mAh). Motors need enough voltage to spin and enough current to push a robot. If voltage is too low, the robot feels weak or stalls. If current available is too small, the battery might overheat or the robot resets.

Common battery types include alkaline (like AA), rechargeable NiMH, and lithium-ion or lithium-polymer (Li-ion/LiPo). Rechargeables are great for classrooms because you can reuse them and keep power steady. Li-ion/LiPo pack a lot of energy in small size but must be handled carefully with proper chargers and adult supervision. Always follow safety rules: never puncture batteries, avoid short circuits (touching positive and negative directly), and store packs safely.

Polarity matters. Battery packs have positive (+) and negative (–) ends. Reverse polarity can damage electronics. Many kits use keyed connectors to prevent mistakes, but if you wire your own, color-code wires and double-check before powering on. Add a switch so you can safely turn power off when adjusting parts.

Power distribution means sharing energy to all components. If motors draw big currents, sensors or microcontrollers might get glitchy. You can separate motor power from logic power, use proper voltage regulators, and add capacitors (tiny “energy buckets”) to smooth spikes. Kids don’t need the deep electronics yet; just remember that clean power makes stable robots.

Now structure. The chassis is the robot’s body—the frame that holds motors, wheels, sensors, and the battery. Good chassis design keeps parts secure, protects fragile sensors, and leaves room for adjustments. Materials might be cardboard, foam board, plastic, wood, or metal. Cardboard is fast and cheap for prototypes; plastic and wood are more durable; metal is strong but heavier and harder to shape.

Load paths describe how forces travel through your robot. If a heavy battery sits on a flimsy platform, bumps can crack it. Use plates and braces to spread forces. Triangles make strong shapes in frames; L-brackets and crossbars add stiffness. Keep weight low and centered to reduce tipping.

Fasteners hold everything together. Screws and nuts are secure; zip ties are quick and flexible; glue is easy but can be hard to adjust later. For parts that need to move, use hinges or axles with bearings so they rotate smoothly without wobble.

Cable management keeps wires safe and tidy. Route wires along the frame, away from wheels and gears. Leave some slack so turning parts don’t tug hard—this is called strain relief. Label connectors so you can reassemble quickly after repairs.

Balance and stability prevent tipping. Picture a robot turning fast with a tall sensor mast—it might lean and fall. Lower heavy parts, widen the wheelbase, and add support wheels if needed. Test on ramps, turns, and bumps so you learn where stability breaks.

Build quality matters. Straight axles reduce wobble. Aligned wheels keep the robot from drifting. Secure mounts stop sensors from vibrating. Taking time to tighten fasteners and align parts makes programming easier because the robot behaves consistently.

Before big tests, make a checklist: battery charged, wires secure, wheels tight, sensors clean, switch off before carrying, clear area of pets and fingers. A simple pre-flight check prevents accidents and saves time.

### Try-It Ideas
- Build two chassis: one heavy and high, one low and wide. Drive both around a sharp turn and compare tipping.
- Practice cable management: route wires with tape or clips, add slack near moving parts, and label connectors.
- Power experiment: test fresh vs. weak batteries; record speed and turning differences.

### Questions
1) Why does keeping heavy components low in the chassis improve stability?
2) What are voltage, current, and capacity, and how do they affect a robot’s performance?
3) Describe two good cable management practices and why they matter.

## **7. Ethics & Real-World Robotics**
- Helpful vs. harmful uses of robots
- Jobs robots do (and how people work with them)
- Teamwork, creativity, and sharing ideas

### Overview
Robotics is not just about building machines—it’s about making choices that affect people, animals, and the environment. Ethics are the rules and values we use to decide what’s right and kind. Real-world robotics asks us to build helpful tools, respect privacy, include everyone, and protect our planet. Kids can understand and apply these ideas from the very beginning.

Helpful uses of robots are everywhere. In hospitals, robots deliver medicine or allow doctors to talk to patients remotely. On farms, robots help plant and harvest while using less water. In disaster zones, robots search dangerous areas where humans shouldn’t go. At home, robots can assist elderly family members by reminding them to take medicine or calling for help.

Harmful uses happen when robots invade privacy, spread misinformation, or put people at risk. A robot with a camera should ask permission before recording. A robot that moves fast near people should slow down and have clear safety rules. We should think about fairness: if a robot makes decisions that affect people (like who gets help first), those rules must be fair and transparent.

Privacy and data protection matter even in kid projects. If your robot collects pictures or sound, talk with adults about where that data goes and how long you keep it. Avoid posting other people’s faces or voices online without permission.

Accessibility means designing robots everyone can use. Big buttons, clear labels, friendly sounds, and adjustable speeds make robots easier for different ages and abilities. If your robot helps in a classroom, include features that help classmates who may have vision or hearing differences.

Environmental care is part of robotics ethics. Choose rechargeable batteries and recycle old ones. Build sturdy robots that last instead of throwaway gadgets. Think about energy use: can your robot do the job using less power? Can you reuse parts and packaging to reduce waste?

Jobs and teamwork in robotics show how people and robots work together. Robots can handle repetitive, risky tasks while humans provide creativity, empathy, and big-picture thinking. In your team, assign roles: builder, coder, tester, documenter. Practice listening, sharing, and celebrating wins together. When something breaks, treat it as a chance to learn, not to blame.

As artificial intelligence (AI) appears in more robots, kids can understand basic ideas: AI helps robots spot patterns (like recognizing a red square), but AI can make mistakes and must be supervised. Set rules like “If unsure, ask a human” or “Always stop if someone says ‘stop.’” Safety, fairness, and kindness guide AI use.

Real-world case studies are powerful. Study a hospital delivery robot: what does it sense, how does it plan routes, how does it avoid people, and what are its safety rules? Or explore a Mars rover: how does it communicate with Earth, protect itself from harsh conditions, and make choices when no human is nearby? When you look closely, you’ll see sense–think–act and clear ethics in action.

Finally, share your work. Write short reports, make posters, create demo videos, or host a mini science fair. Describe how your robot helps, your safety rules, and what you learned. Teaching others spreads good robotics values.

### Try-It Ideas
- Create a “robot rules” poster: privacy, safety, kindness, accessibility, and environment.
- Redesign a classroom robot with bigger buttons, slower speed, and clear sounds; test with friends and gather feedback.
- Investigate a real robot (home, school, online) and list its helpful features and safety rules.

### Questions
1) Why is privacy important in robotics projects that use cameras or microphones?
2) Give two ways to make a robot more accessible to different users.
3) Describe a helpful real-world robot and the safety rules it follows.
